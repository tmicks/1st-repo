## Module 1 — Financial Assessment

### Personal Financial Snapshot

> You can't build a plan from a guess. Before we touch a budget, a credit report, or a banking pattern, we need one honest snapshot of where things stand today — no judgment, just facts.
>
> A Personal Financial Snapshot has four parts: what's coming in (all income sources, even irregular ones), what's going out (every recurring obligation, not just the big ones), what you have saved (even $40 counts), and what you owe (every debt, every account, listed — this is not the moment to leave anything off because it's uncomfortable).
>
> Most people underestimate their expenses and overestimate their savings buffer. That's normal — it's exactly why this exercise exists. Once this snapshot is done, your coach will use it to help you build your first real budget in the next lesson.
>
> This snapshot becomes the baseline your coach compares against at your Midpoint Assessment, so accuracy now matters more than looking good now.

### Budget Builder

> Now that you have your Personal Financial Snapshot, it's time to turn it into a working monthly budget.
>
> A budget isn't a restriction — it's a plan for where every dollar goes before you spend it. Start with your real numbers from the Snapshot, not the numbers you wish were true. A budget built on wishful thinking breaks in the first week.
>
> **The 3-Bucket Method** — assign every dollar of income into one of three buckets:
> - **Needs** — rent, utilities, groceries, minimum debt payments
> - **Obligations** — anything you're committed to paying (loans, subscriptions, support payments)
> - **Discretionary** — everything else, including savings
>
> Build in a buffer, even if it's just $20 a month. A budget with zero slack breaks at the first surprise expense — and there's always a surprise expense.
>
> This is a draft. You'll revisit and refine it again in Module 4 once your credit and banking work is done, so don't aim for perfect right now — aim for real.

### Income Analysis

> Before you can manage money, you need a complete and honest picture of what's actually coming in.
>
> List every source of income you have — your primary job, side income, benefits, child support, gig work. Lenders and landlords will ask about all of it later in this program, so get comfortable listing it now rather than leaving anything out.
>
> **Rate each source:**
> - **Guaranteed** — you can count on it every period
> - **Likely** — usually there, but not guaranteed
> - **Variable** — unpredictable in timing or amount
>
> This matters more for your budget than the total number does. A "guaranteed" $2,000 behaves very differently than a "variable" $2,000.
>
> Also note the timing of each source — weekly, biweekly, monthly. Mismatched timing between income and bills is one of the most common hidden causes of a shortfall, even when there's technically enough money coming in over the month.
>
> If there's a gap between your current income and what you'll need to hit your stated goal from your Beginning Assessment, write it down here — that becomes a conversation with your coach, not something to solve alone in this lesson.

### Expense Analysis

> Most people underestimate what they actually spend — usually by 20-30%, because memory just isn't a reliable record of spending.
>
> Pull your last 60-90 days of actual transactions — bank and card statements, not memory — and sort every expense into one of three categories:
> - **Fixed** — the same amount every month (rent, car payment, insurance)
> - **Variable-necessary** — changes month to month, but has to happen (groceries, gas, utilities)
> - **Variable-discretionary** — optional, or at least flexible (entertainment, dining out, subscriptions)
>
> **Find the leaks:** look specifically for small, recurring charges that add up without being noticed — old subscriptions, fees, memberships you forgot about. These "leak" categories are usually the easiest place to find real money without changing your lifestyle.
>
> Pick 1-2 realistic reduction targets from what you find — not five aggressive ones. The goal is a plan you'll actually follow next month, not a perfect plan you abandon in a week.

## Module 2 — Credit Support

### Understanding Credit

> A credit score isn't a judgment about who you are — it's a number lenders and landlords use to estimate the likelihood that you'll repay a debt as agreed.
>
> Three major bureaus — Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion — each hold slightly different data on you, which is why your score can vary depending on where it's pulled from.
>
> **Why this module matters for you specifically:** your credit directly affects what Tier 2 (Housing) and Tier 3 (Entrepreneur) can offer you later — the interest rate on a mortgage, whether a landlord approves your application, the terms a business lender will give you. This module exists because those later doors depend on the work you do here.
>
> Different score ranges unlock different things. There's no single "good" number that matters everywhere; what matters is steady improvement from wherever you're starting.

### Credit Factors

> Your credit score is built from five factors, and they don't count equally.
>
> - **Payment history (~35%)** — the single biggest factor. On-time payments matter more than anything else on this list.
> - **Amounts owed / utilization (~30%)** — covered in depth in its own lesson later this module.
> - **Length of credit history (~15%)** — why closing your oldest account can hurt more than it helps.
> - **Credit mix (~10%)** — having both revolving credit (cards) and installment loans helps a little, but it's never worth opening new debt just to diversify.
> - **New credit / inquiries (~10%)** — a hard inquiry has a small, temporary impact. Rate-shopping for a mortgage or auto loan within a short window typically counts as a single inquiry, not several.
>
> Knowing the weight of each factor helps you focus your energy where it actually moves the number — which, for almost everyone, starts with payment history and utilization.

### Reviewing Credit Reports

> You can't fix what you haven't looked at. This lesson is about actually pulling and reading all three of your bureau reports.
>
> Use annualcreditreport.com (or your program's designated tool) to pull your reports for free — you don't need a paid monitoring service to do this work.
>
> **What's actually on a report:** your accounts, payment history, inquiries, and any public records — but not your score itself, which is a separate pull.
>
> **What to look for:**
> - A balance that looks wrong
> - An account you don't recognize
> - A payment marked late that you're confident was on time
>
> **Bring to your next lesson:** a copy — printed or saved — of all three reports, with anything that looks off clearly marked. That annotated copy is exactly what the next two lessons build on.

### Understanding Derogatory Accounts

> A derogatory account is anything on your report that signals a debt wasn't paid as agreed — and this lesson exists so those words stop being scary and start being manageable.
>
> - **Collection** — an unpaid debt that's been sold or assigned to a collection agency.
> - **Charge-off** — the original creditor has written the debt off as a loss for their own accounting, but the debt itself can still exist and may still be collected.
> - **Judgment** — a court has ordered payment.
>
> These generally stay on a report for around 7 years regardless of whether they're ever paid — paying doesn't erase the history, though it can change how the item is shown.
>
> **Your options:** dispute it if it's inaccurate, negotiate a settlement or pay-for-delete (results aren't guaranteed, but it's worth asking), or let it age off if neither of those is realistic right now.
>
> If a formal dispute is warranted, that's where your coach brings in staff-side casework — your job in this lesson is identifying what's there, not handling the dispute process yourself.

### Understanding Utilization

> Utilization is the percentage of your available credit you're currently using, and it's one of the fastest levers you can pull to improve your score.
>
> **The math:** utilization = balance ÷ credit limit — calculated both per card and across all your revolving accounts combined.
>
> **The target:** under 30% is generally considered acceptable. Under 10% is strong. There's no cliff exactly at 30% — lower is simply better, all the way down.
>
> **Two ways to move it:**
> 1. Pay down balances.
> 2. Don't close old accounts with available limit — closing an account reduces your total available credit, which can raise your utilization even if you haven't spent a dime more.
>
> One more thing worth knowing: bureaus typically calculate utilization based on your statement closing date, not your due date. Paying down a balance right before the statement closes — not just before it's due — can get a lower utilization reported faster.

### Credit Action Plan

> This lesson pulls together everything from Module 2 into one plan you'll actually work.
>
> **Build your plan:**
> 1. List the derogatory items you're addressing, and how (dispute, negotiate, or let age off).
> 2. Note your current utilization versus your target.
> 3. Flag any quick wins you found along the way.
>
> Pick your 2-3 highest-impact actions for the next 90 days — not a 15-item list that never gets finished. Prioritization beats volume every time.
>
> Your coach reviews this plan with you, and it becomes the tracked record for your credit-related milestones. If disputes are needed, this is the hand-off point to staff-side credit repair casework — you'll continue into Module 3 in parallel, not wait on the outcome of any dispute.

## Module 3 — Banking Alignment

### Why Banking Matters

> A consistent banking relationship is something landlords and lenders read as evidence of financial behavior that a credit report alone doesn't show.
>
> It's not just about the balance in the account — it's about the pattern. Regular deposits, manageable overdrafts (ideally none), and a history they can actually see.
>
> This module is preparing you for paperwork you'll need in both Tier 2 (income verification for housing) and Tier 3 (business banking) — not just for right now.
>
> If you're currently unbanked or rely mainly on check-cashing services or prepaid cards, that's not a character issue — it's a practical gap this module exists to close, without judgment.

>

### Deposit Consistency

> Lenders and landlords read consistent deposits as a sign of income stability — irregular, unpredictable deposits raise questions even when the total amount coming in is enough.
>
> If your income is irregular or comes from gig work, the single highest-leverage move in this lesson is to batch your deposits on a fixed schedule — for example, every Friday — rather than depositing sporadically as cash comes in.
>
> Set up direct deposit wherever you can. It's the simplest way to build the pattern this lesson is about.

### Cash Flow Management

> Most "shortfalls" aren't income problems — they're timing problems. This lesson is about managing the gap between when money comes in and when it has to go out.
>
> Map your income dates against your bill due dates on one calendar. Once you can see the whole month at once, timing conflicts that felt like a mystery usually become obvious.
>
> **Ways to close a timing gap:**
> - Ask a creditor to shift a due date
> - Build a one-pay-period buffer so you're never paying next month's bill with next month's paycheck
> - Use your Financial Calendar (Module 4) to see the full picture at a glance
>
> Reducing this kind of timing risk also protects the banking relationship you're building in this module — overdrafts and late fees work against you here just as much as they do with a lender.

### Documentation

> This module closes with what to actually save, because Tier 2 and Tier 3 are both going to ask you for it.
>
> - **Tier 2 (Housing):** typically 2-3 months of bank statements for income verification.
> - **Tier 3 (Business):** a separate business account and its statements — kept distinct from personal, never commingled, starting from day one of business banking.
>
> Download or export your statements now and organize them somewhere you can find them quickly. A few minutes now saves real time when a document request lands later in the program.

## Module 4 — Financial Stability Plan

### Budget

> Time to revisit the budget you drafted back in Module 1, now with everything you've learned since then folded in — your credit action plan, your banking-aligned cash flow timing.
>
> This isn't a brand-new exercise. It's a refinement of the work you already did, updated with real numbers instead of early-program estimates.
>
> This finalized version is what your coach signs off on as your Tier 1 completion artifact.

### Savings

> Start with a baseline emergency fund goal — even one month of expenses is a meaningful target — and then set a second, track-specific goal: a security deposit or down payment if you're headed toward Tier 2, or startup capital if you're headed toward Tier 3.
>
> Automate whatever you can. A recurring transfer that happens whether or not you remember beats a manual one every time.

### Financial Calendar

> Bring together everything from this Tier into one recurring calendar: your income timing (Module 1), your bill due dates (Cash Flow Management), and a monthly date to review it all.
>
> The calendar itself is just scaffolding. The real habit this lesson is installing is the monthly review — a fixed date where you check in with your own numbers instead of letting a month go by on autopilot.

### Financial Milestones

> This is where you and your coach agree on the specific milestones that get tracked in your case record and shown on your dashboard — things like "credit action plan implemented," "utilization under target," "emergency fund at $X," or "banking documentation ready."
>
> Once these are defined and reviewed live with your coach, completing them is what triggers your **Financial Stability Certificate** and unlocks **Tier 2 — Housing Access & Stability**.
>
> This is the last lesson in Tier 1 — take the time to make sure these milestones actually reflect where you are, since your coach will be checking progress against exactly what's written here.
