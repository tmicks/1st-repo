## Module 1 — Business Formation

### Choosing an Entity

> Before you can bank, hire, or take on capital as a business, you need to choose a legal structure — and this lesson walks through the common options in plain language.
>
> **The Common Options**
> - Sole proprietorship — simplest to set up, but offers no separation between you and the business
> - LLC — the common default for most first-time business owners in this program, because it separates your personal assets from business liability without the complexity of a corporation
> - S-corp / corporation — more complex, usually relevant once a business reaches a certain size or has specific tax needs
>
> Most participants in this program start with an LLC, unless a specific licensing or contractor requirement points to a different structure — your coach can help you confirm which applies to you.
>
> This program educates you on these options — it doesn't replace legal or tax advice. Confirm your specific choice with a qualified attorney or CPA before filing.

### LLC

> Once you've chosen an LLC, here's what actually filing one looks like.
>
> **The Standard Steps**
> 1. Choose and reserve your business name
> 2. File Articles of Organization with your state
> 3. Pay the filing fee
> 4. Obtain any licenses or permits required for your specific type of business
>
> Filing fees and processing times vary by state — *[link to your state's Secretary of State filing portal here]*.
>
> You'll also need a registered agent — someone who can receive legal documents on the business's behalf. Your coach can walk you through the options for meeting this requirement if you don't already have one in mind.

### EIN

> An EIN — Employer Identification Number — is what lets you open a business bank account and file business taxes, whether or not you plan to ever hire an employee.
>
> Getting one is free and takes about 15 minutes directly through IRS.gov. If you see a paid "EIN filing service," skip it — there's no reason to pay for something the IRS provides for free.
>
> One thing to watch for: apply for your EIN only after your LLC filing is confirmed. Applying too early can create a mismatch between your business name and your registration.

### Operating Agreement

> Even if you're the only owner of your LLC, an operating agreement is worth having.
>
> **What It Covers**
> - Ownership percentages
> - Decision-making authority
> - What happens if you bring on a partner, or if the business dissolves
>
> For a single-member LLC, this document reinforces the separation between you and the business that makes your liability protection actually mean something in practice.
>
> It doesn't get filed with the state, but keep it with your business records — banks and future capital partners may ask to see it.

### Business Checklist

> This lesson closes out Module 1 by confirming everything is actually in place before you move to Business Banking.
>
> **Confirm You Have:**
> ☐ Entity chosen
> ☐ LLC filed and confirmed
> ☐ EIN obtained
> ☐ Operating agreement drafted
> ☐ Any required licenses or permits secured
>
> Your coach reviews this checklist with you before Module 2 unlocks.

## Module 2 — Business Banking

### Business Accounts

> With your formation paperwork complete, it's time to open a dedicated business bank account.
>
> Keeping business and personal money separate isn't just good practice — commingling funds undermines the liability protection your LLC exists to provide, and makes bookkeeping and future lending applications far harder.
>
> To open a business account, you'll typically need your EIN, formation documents, operating agreement, and personal ID — everything you built in Module 1. If your program's banking partnership extends to business accounts, ask your coach whether that option applies to you.

### Recordkeeping

> A basic, sustainable recordkeeping system is worth more than an elaborate one you abandon after a month.
>
> At minimum, track every income and expense with a receipt or invoice trail — a spreadsheet is enough to start. Separate categories that matter for tax time from day one, rather than trying to reconstruct them later.
>
> Consistency beats sophistication here. A simple system you actually use every week outperforms a complex one you touch once a quarter.

### Accounting Basics

> A few core concepts make it possible to actually read your own numbers.
>
> **Three numbers new business owners often confuse:**
> - Revenue — total money coming in
> - Profit — what's left after expenses
> - Cash on hand — what's actually available right now, which can lag behind both of the above
>
> A basic income statement — revenue minus expenses equals profit — read monthly, not just at tax time, is the habit this lesson is building.
>
> There's a point where bringing in a bookkeeper or accountant makes more sense than continuing to self-manage — ask your coach what threshold makes sense for your specific business as it grows.

### Cash Flow

> Business cash flow is often more irregular than personal income — invoices lag, demand is seasonal, materials cost money upfront before a project pays out.
>
> Build a cash reserve sized to your specific business's volatility, not a generic number pulled from a personal-finance rule of thumb.
>
> The trap this lesson exists to help you avoid: being profitable on paper while being cash-poor in practice, because of timing rather than a real lack of money.

## Module 3 — Business Capital Pathways

### Understanding Business Credit

> Business credit is tracked separately from your personal credit — through bureaus like Dun & Bradstreet, Experian Business, and Equifax Business — and building it protects your personal credit from business risk.
>
> To start building it, your entity and EIN (Module 1) and your business bank account (Module 2) need to already be in place.
>
> Early steps include net-30 vendor accounts (next lesson), a business credit card used and paid consistently, and on-time payment to any trade accounts you open.

### Vendor Accounts

> A net-30 vendor account lets you buy now and pay in 30 days — and when the vendor reports that payment history to a business credit bureau, it becomes one of the fastest ways to start building business credit.
>
> Look for vendors relevant to your actual business, not accounts opened just for the sake of having them. Your coach can help you find ones in your specific industry that report to the bureaus.
>
> Payment discipline matters here exactly as much as it did with your personal credit work in Tier 1 — the mechanics are different, the behavior isn't.

### Financing Basics

> Once you need capital beyond what vendor credit provides, here's the landscape of common options.
>
> - **SBA loans** — government-backed, often more accessible for newer businesses
> - **Microloans** — smaller amounts, frequently a good fit for early-stage businesses
> - **Business lines of credit** — flexible, revolving access to capital
> - **Project-based financing** — particularly relevant for clean energy contractors, connects forward to Module 4
>
> Lenders typically look at your business credit, your personal credit (still relevant for newer businesses), your cash flow history, time in business, and any collateral available.
>
> **Avoid** high-cost merchant cash advances and similar predatory financing — ask your coach if you're ever unsure whether an offer is a fair one.

### Responsible Capital Management

> Getting capital is only half the equation — using it without jeopardizing the business is the other half.
>
> Match the type of financing to its purpose: a short-term operating gap shouldn't be covered with long-term debt, and a long-term investment shouldn't rely on short-term financing.
>
> Before taking on any new payment obligation, ask whether your actual cash flow (Module 2) can support the payback — not whether it looks manageable on paper.
>
> Over-leveraging looks like taking on more debt than your cash flow supports "just in case" — this lesson is about catching that pattern early, before it becomes a real problem.

### Capital Planning

> This lesson pulls Module 3 together into one plan: what capital you actually need, for what specific purpose, from what likely source, and on what timeline.
>
> Your coach reviews this plan with you before you move into Module 4 (if you're pursuing the Illinois Shines / EEP-EEC path) or before your Program Completion evaluation, if your track ends here.

## Module 4 — Illinois Shines

### Illinois Shines Overview

> Illinois Shines is the state's solar renewable energy credit (SREC) program, and this module exists specifically for participants pursuing clean energy contractor work.
>
> This module builds directly on the business formation and capital work you completed in Modules 1-3 — it's a specific project and capital pipeline layered on top of a business you've already built the foundation for, not a separate track that skips the basics.
>
> *[Verify current Illinois Shines program details against the program's official materials before publishing — incentive program terms change.]*

### EEP

> An Equity Eligible Person (EEP) is a defined status within the Illinois Shines program, and this lesson covers what that status means and whether you qualify.
>
> *[Confirm current EEP qualifying criteria against official Illinois Shines program documentation.]*
>
> EEP status is the pathway toward EEC status, covered next, and toward specific training and apprenticeship opportunities within the clean energy contractor pipeline. Your coach can help document your EEP status for program and funder reporting purposes.

### EEC

> An Equity Eligible Contractor (EEC) is the next status along this pathway, building on EEP status.
>
> *[Confirm current EEC requirements against official Illinois Shines program documentation.]*
>
> EEC status is what unlocks contractor participation in the Illinois Shines project pipeline (next lesson) and eligibility for the EEC Incubator (Module 5) — this is the specific outcome your program tracks as "EEP → EEC Advancement."

### Contractor Participation

> Here's what it actually looks like to operate as a contractor inside Illinois Shines.
>
> Projects get assigned or bid within the program's structure, and contractors are expected to meet specific compliance and reporting requirements.
>
> This is where everything from Modules 2 and 3 gets used in practice — business banking, recordkeeping, cash flow management, and capital. If you skipped ahead to this module without that foundation, you'll feel the gap here.

### Project Pipeline

> Once you're an active EEC, this lesson covers how to actually access and evaluate available projects.
>
> Evaluate every project against your real business capacity — crew size, equipment, cash flow runway — rather than taking on more than the business can currently deliver.
>
> If this evaluation shows your capacity isn't quite there yet, that's exactly what Module 5's Incubator exists to help you build.

## Module 5 — EEC Incubator

### What Is an Incubator?

> The EEC Incubator provides structured support — mentorship, capacity-building, project readiness coaching — for EECs who aren't yet ready to operate fully independently in the project pipeline.
>
> It's for contractors who've completed EEC status but assessed, in the Project Pipeline lesson, that their capacity isn't yet sufficient for independent project participation.
>
> Expect a real time commitment here — this module is about building toward "graduating" the incubator with genuine readiness, not just completing lessons.

### Business Capacity

> Building capacity starts with an honest assessment of where you actually stand.
>
> **Dimensions to assess:**
> - Crew size and skill
> - Equipment
> - Bonding and insurance capacity
> - Cash flow runway to cover a project before payment arrives
>
> Assess against real project requirements from the pipeline, not aspirational capacity. From there, build a capacity-growth plan with concrete, sequenced steps — not a vague goal to "grow the business."

### Mentorship

> As part of the Incubator, you'll be matched with a mentor.
>
> Ask your coach what the mentor relationship actually involves — how often you'll meet, what's in scope, and what a mentor can and can't do for you.
>
> Get the most out of it by bringing specific, current business questions to each session rather than open-ended check-ins.

### Project Readiness

> This lesson brings together your capacity assessment and your mentorship progress into an actual readiness determination.
>
> Readiness isn't all-or-nothing — you might come out of this ready for smaller projects but not yet larger ones. That's a normal, useful outcome, not a failure to graduate.

### Scaling

> This is the final lesson of Tier 3 for EEP → EEC track participants — and it's about what comes after the Incubator.
>
> Scaling requires three things working together: additional capital (Module 3), additional crew or capacity (this module's Business Capacity lesson), and continued experience taking on pipeline projects.
>
> Completing this lesson alongside the rest of Modules 1-5 is what triggers your coach's sign-off for the **Entrepreneur & Economic Participation Certificate**. If this is also your final Tier, it's what triggers your **Economic Stability Certificate** — Program Completion — and the point where Phase 2 academy options come into the conversation.
